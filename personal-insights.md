## Core Patterns

### The Manager Part (IFS)
Keeps plates spinning to prevent ever feeling the childhood pain underneath.
Believes: *"As long as everything is under control, we won't have to feel that."*
Loyal. Exhausted. Still running a program written for a child who no longer needs it.

### Over-functioning / Under-communicating
Taking everything on → can't ask for help → resentment builds → needs come out sideways
as emotion → others don't respond cleanly → confirms "I have to do it myself."

### Need to Be Liked / Rejection Sensitivity
Inherited from mum's emotional architecture. Amplified by ADHD (RSD).
Love in childhood wasn't unconditional — it required management.

### Wounded Helping / Savior Pattern
Gift emerged from dysfunction: reading the emotional room to stay safe.
The wound: worth feels contingent on the helping working.
The shift: help from genuine care, not from anxiety. Offer it, don't own the outcome.

### Self-Sabotage When Building for Yourself
Spiral triggers specifically when pursuing something *for you* — not for others.
Porn, drinking, creating drama: dopamine substitutes + escape from vulnerability.
Half-trying protects from the verdict: *"See, you were unrealistic. You don't deserve it."*

# Why You Take Shortcuts & Only Do the Big Visible Things

## What's actually happening

You don't finish things. You do the dramatic, noticeable parts well and stall on
the invisible completion work. This feels like laziness. It isn't.

## The ADHD layer

ADHD brains run on an interest-based nervous system. Activation comes from:
- **Novelty** — the start of something new
- **Urgency** — high stakes, deadline pressure
- **Challenge** — something hard enough to be engaging
- **Passion** — genuine emotional investment

The big visible parts of any project hit most of these. The completion work —
the admin, the follow-through, the maintenance, the invisible systems — hits none of them.

The dopamine that drove you to start is genuinely gone before the finish line.
This isn't a motivation problem. It's neurological. Your brain physically cannot
sustain the same engagement on low-stimulation repetitive work without external structure.

## The psychological layer

### Visible work = validation
Given your history — not being seen, having to earn your place — doing noticeable
things that get acknowledged feeds the wound. The invisible work gets no response.
No response = no proof you matter. So the nervous system doesn't prioritise it.

### Starting = hope. Finishing = risk.
When something is unfinished, it can still be everything you imagined.
The moment it's complete, it can be judged. And if it's judged and found lacking —
the childhood verdict activates: *"See. You were unrealistic."*

Half-finishing is unconscious self-protection. You can't fail at something you never completed.

### The "not lazy" performance
You were called lazy. That landed as shame.
Doing the big, dramatic, visible parts quickly is a performance of effort —
proof to yourself and others that you're not that. The boring completion work
doesn't carry that redemptive quality. So it doesn't get done.

### The Manager and novelty
The Manager part loves a new project. A new project is controllable, exciting,
full of potential. The grind of completion requires tolerating uncertainty,
tedium, and the vulnerability of sustained effort. The Manager would rather
start something new — which feels productive — than sit with the discomfort
of finishing something old.

## Where this shows up in the business

- The concept, pitch, and initial build: energised, capable, fast
- The follow-through, systems, consistency, admin: where it dies
- The invisible work that compounds over time (content, outreach, refinement):
  perpetually deferred
- The "nearly there" stage: most dangerous — close enough to be judged, far enough to abandon

## What actually helps

**1. Make the invisible work visible**
Break completion tasks into units that can be *seen* as done. A checklist.
A Kanban column. Anything that creates the external signal your brain can't generate internally.

**2. External accountability for the boring bits**
You finish things for others that you don't finish for yourself.
Build in a person — not just a deadline. Someone who sees your output,
not just your intentions.

**3. Name the protection when it activates**
When you stall on completion, ask: *"What am I protecting myself from
by not finishing this?"* Usually it's judgment. Or confirmation of the old verdict.
Naming it breaks the unconscious operation slightly.

**4. Minimum viable completion**
The invisible work doesn't have to be perfect. It has to exist.
A draft sent is infinitely more useful than a perfect draft in your head.
Define what "complete enough" means before you start, so the Manager
can't keep moving the goalposts.

**5. Separate "done" from "good"**
Done is a fact. Good is a judgment. You need done. Good is optional and iterative.
The ADHD + shame combination turns every completion into a referendum on your worth.
It isn't. It's just a thing that got finished.
