## Entry 001 — First Nations FPP Specialist
**Date:** April 2026
**Source:** Organic conversation (platform not recorded)
**Segment:** First Nations child and family services — specialist worker

### What they do
- Specialist for Family Preservation Program (FPP) — developed through Queensland legislative amendments in 2017 for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander families
- Advocacy for rights to self-determination and agency
- Holding government departments accountable to their own legislation and practice standards
- Building culturally grounded capacity with First Nations families ("Mob")
- Empowering families to exercise their rights
- Guiding families through service support
- Mitigating risk to children ("Jarjums") and preventing removal

### Pain points identified
- **Admin is a significant burden** — case noting and reporting described as "heavy"
- Acknowledged the admin load is proportionate to the seriousness and risk involved, so it's unavoidable — not just inefficiency
- Strong preference for frontline work; admin is the thing they do *despite* loving the work, not because of it
- Implicit: the emotional and cognitive load of the work is high — admin on top of that is the friction point

### What they love
- The frontline work itself
- Structure and procedures (not averse to process — just to unnecessary admin)
- Creating and empowering positive impact for Mob in community
- Culturally safe and responsive service delivery

### Signals for BWA
- Clear product-market fit on admin reduction framing: *"reduce the admin so you can stay in the work that matters"*
- Values alignment is strong — mission-driven, empowerment-focused, not transactional
- This person would respond to peer respect and genuine understanding of the space, not a cold pitch

## Segment Notes — First Nations Family Services

### Opportunity
- Genuine admin burden in high-stakes, legislatively complex work
- Underserved by mainstream tech/AI tools
- Strong appetite for anything that frees up time for frontline work

### Risks and considerations for BWA
- **Data sovereignty is non-negotiable.** First Nations organisations and programs operate under specific data governance frameworks (AIATSIS Code of Ethics, CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance). Any AI tools must be compliant and community-controlled.
- **Cultural safety extends to the tools themselves** — not just how you deliver training. Needs to be part of the offer design, not an afterthought.
- **Trust is earned slowly in this space.** Cold outreach is unlikely to work. Relationships through trusted intermediaries (e.g., ATSICHS, QATSICPP, peak bodies) are the right entry point.
- **Government accountability angle is significant.** These workers hold departments to account — they'll scrutinise any tool or process that touches client data or case records carefully.

### BWA positioning recommendation
- Do NOT lead with this segment
- Build 1–2 general NFP case studies first
- Approach through First Nations peak bodies (QATSICPP, SNAICC nationally) rather than cold
- When ready: lead with admin reduction + cultural safety, not AI capability
- Frame: *"More time with families, less time at the keyboard"*

### Relevant peak bodies for future outreach
- **SNAICC** (Secretariat of National Aboriginal and Islander Child Care) — national peak for First Nations children and families
- **QATSICPP** (Queensland Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Child Protection Peak) — QLD-specific
- **ATSICHS** (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Community Health Service) — Brisbane-based, community-controlled
